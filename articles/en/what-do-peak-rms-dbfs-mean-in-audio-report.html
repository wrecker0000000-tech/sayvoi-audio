What Do Peak, RMS and dBFS Mean in an Audio Quality Report?

Last updated: November 27, 2026

An audio quality report shows numbers such as Peak, RMS and dBFS. They look technical, but each one answers a simple question about your recording. This guide explains them in plain words.

dBFS: the scale

dBFS means decibels relative to full scale. It is the scale used for digital audio. 0 dBFS is the loudest value a digital file can hold. Every normal value is negative, and the closer a number is to 0, the louder the sound.

Peak: the loudest spike

Peak is the highest single point in the recording. It tells you how close the sound came to the limit. A peak around -6 to -3 dBFS is a safe, healthy level for speech. A peak at 0 dBFS means the sound may be clipped, which is distortion you cannot fix later. A very low peak, such as -22 dBFS, means the voice is quiet.

RMS: the average volume

RMS is the average loudness over time. It is closer to what your ears hear than the peak. For speech, an RMS of about -20 to -16 dBFS is common. If the RMS is much lower, the voice sounds weak and the client may ask you to record again.

Noise floor: the quietest part

The noise floor is the level of the quietest part, usually the pauses between words. The lower it is, the cleaner the recording. A noise floor below -50 dBFS is good for most projects. A higher value means you can hear background noise.

How to read your report

  • Peak -5, RMS -18: a good recording.
  • Peak -22: the voice is too quiet. Move closer to the microphone or raise the input level, then record again.
  • Peak 0: the sound is clipped. Lower the input level and record again.
  • Noise floor -40: there is audible noise. Record in a quieter room.
