Bit Depth Explained: 16-bit vs 24-bit vs 32-bit Float

Last updated: October 24, 2026

Sample rate tells you how many times per second the sound is measured. Bit depth tells you how finely each measurement is stored. More bits means a finer scale between the quietest and loudest value.

What each bit depth gives you

Bit depthTheoretical dynamic rangeTypical use
8-bitabout 48 dBOld or very low-quality audio. Audible hiss.
16-bitabout 96 dBCDs, most speech datasets, general delivery.
24-bitabout 144 dBRecording and editing. Lots of headroom.
32-bit floatvery large (floating point)Internal processing and some recorders. Can hold levels above 0 dBFS inside the file.

In practice, no microphone or room is quiet enough to use all 144 dB of a 24-bit file. The benefit of 24-bit is not that it sounds better when played back. It is that you can record at a safer, lower level without losing quality, because the noise from the quantization is far below the noise of the room.

File size

Uncompressed size is easy to calculate: sample rate × bytes per sample × channels × seconds.

  • 16 kHz, mono, 16-bit: 32,000 bytes per second, about 1.9 MB per minute.
  • 48 kHz, mono, 24-bit: 144,000 bytes per second, about 8.6 MB per minute.
  • 44.1 kHz, stereo, 16-bit: 176,400 bytes per second, about 10.6 MB per minute.

Which to choose

  • Delivering speech data: follow the project specification. 16-bit is the most common requirement, and it is plenty for speech.
  • Recording: use 24-bit if your device offers it, then convert to the required depth at the end.
  • 32-bit float: useful if you cannot control levels, because the recording will not clip inside the file. Convert it to 16 or 24-bit for delivery and check the level first.

A note on conversion

Reducing bit depth, for example from 24 to 16, throws away the finest detail. Professional tools add a tiny amount of noise called dither to hide the rounding error. Raising bit depth, from 16 to 24, only changes the container: it does not bring back detail that was never recorded. The WAV files that the Sayvoi converter writes are 16-bit, which suits most speech delivery requirements. If a project requires 24-bit, convert with a desktop tool instead.
