WAV vs MP3 vs M4A vs OGG vs FLAC

Last updated: 27 Oct 2026 · about 4 min read

The audio format you pick affects quality, file size and compatibility. This guide explains the common formats and helps you choose. (For a deeper look at just three formats, see WAV vs MP3 vs FLAC.)

Two ideas you need first

  • Lossless compression: the file is smaller, yet the sound is identical to the original once decoded. FLAC is an example.
  • Lossy compression: removes detail that is hard to hear in order to shrink the file a lot. MP3 and AAC are examples. The lower the bitrate, the more is lost.

Also note the difference between the container (the file extension) and the codec (how the sound inside is compressed). For example, M4A is a container, and usually holds AAC audio.

A closer look at each format

WAV

Raw, uncompressed sound. Full quality, but large, and the traditional format has a technical limit of about 4 GB per file. It is the best choice while recording, editing and processing, and when a client asks for a "raw" file.

MP3

The most famous format; almost any device plays it. Quality is very acceptable at 128 to 192 kbps for everyday listening, and higher (256 to 320) if you want more headroom.

M4A (usually AAC)

Often gives better quality than MP3 at the same bitrate, or the same quality at a smaller size. Be careful though: not every M4A is the same kind, and it may contain ALAC (lossless).

OGG

An open, royalty-free format. Quality is good at small sizes, but support is thinner on some devices and apps, so check before relying on it.

FLAC

Keeps the sound identical to the source while reducing size. Excellent for archiving. It is much larger than MP3, so not always the best for sharing.

Important rules

  • Do not convert lossy to lossy again and again. Each pass adds new loss. Keep the original.
  • Converting MP3 to WAV or FLAC does not bring quality back. It only makes the file bigger, because removed detail cannot return.
  • For speech (transcription, calls): a low bitrate (for example 64 to 96 kbps) or a 16 kHz mono sample rate is often enough. See sample rate.

Quick guide to choosing

  • Recording, editing or delivering to a client → WAV
  • Sharing, uploading or playing on any device → MP3
  • Better quality at a small size on modern devices → M4A
  • Games or open-source apps → OGG
  • Full-quality archive, smaller than WAV → FLAC
