How to Reduce Audio File Size Without Ruining Quality

Last updated: 27 Oct 2026 · about 4 min read

A big audio file means slow uploads, more storage and trouble sending it. The good news: you can shrink it a lot, as long as you know which settings affect quality and which barely do.

An example with numbers

A speech recording that is one hour long:

SettingApproximate size
WAV, 44.1 kHz, stereo, 16-bitabout 635 MB
WAV, 16 kHz, mono, 16-bitabout 115 MB
MP3, mono, 64 kbpsabout 29 MB

Same content, and the last option is under 5% of the original size. That suits speech (lectures, meetings, transcription), but not music.

The six ways

1) Pick a suitable compressed format

Going from WAV to MP3 or M4A is the biggest single saving. To compare formats see the formats guide. If you need source quality at a smaller size, try FLAC.

2) Lower the bitrate

A general rule: for speech, 48 to 96 kbps is often fine; for music, 128 to 192 kbps for everyday listening and 256 to 320 for higher quality. Test and compare by ear before committing.

3) Use mono for speech

Most speech recordings are a single voice, so stereo doubles the size for no gain. Converting to mono roughly halves the size.

4) Reduce the sample rate for speech

Human speech usually does not need 44.1 or 48 kHz. 16 kHz or 24 kHz is often enough for clarity. Details in the sample rate article. Do not do this for music, since you would lose high frequencies.

5) Trim silence and unneeded parts

Removing empty starts and ends, or long pauses, saves space with no effect on quality.

6) Do not re-compress more than once

Each lossy-to-lossy conversion adds loss. Keep an original, and convert from it once with the final settings.

Note: zipping files that are already compressed (MP3, M4A) usually saves almost nothing. Shrink through the settings above, and use ZIP only to bundle several files into one.
