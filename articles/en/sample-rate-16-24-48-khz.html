What Is Sample Rate? 16 vs 24 vs 44.1 vs 48 kHz Explained

Last updated: October 24, 2026

The sample rate tells you how many times per second a digital recording measures the sound. It is written in hertz (Hz) or kilohertz (kHz): 16 kHz means 16,000 measurements every second. Choosing the right sample rate affects quality, file size and compatibility with other software.

The key idea: half the sample rate

A digital recording can only capture frequencies up to half its sample rate. This is known as the Nyquist limit. A 16 kHz recording captures up to 8 kHz, a 44.1 kHz recording captures up to 22.05 kHz, and a 48 kHz recording captures up to 24 kHz. Human hearing reaches roughly 20 kHz at best, which is why 44.1 and 48 kHz became the standards for music and video.

Common sample rates and where they are used

22.05 / 24 kHz11 to 12 kHzLower-bandwidth voice, some speech synthesis work
44.1 kHz22.05 kHzAudio CDs and most music
48 kHz24 kHzVideo, broadcast and professional audio

Which one should you choose?

  • Speech for recognition or data collection: 16 kHz is very common, but always follow the specification you were given.
  • Music: 44.1 kHz.
  • Anything that goes into a video: 48 kHz, to match the video workflow.
  • Not sure: keep the original sample rate. You can always convert later.

What happens when you change the sample rate

Lowering the sample rate (for example 48 kHz to 16 kHz) removes the high frequencies above the new limit and makes the file smaller. Raising it (16 kHz to 48 kHz) does not bring back detail that was never recorded: the file only becomes larger. So record at the rate you need or higher, and convert downward once, from the original.

How much space does it take?

For uncompressed 16-bit mono WAV, the size is the sample rate multiplied by 2 bytes. At 16 kHz that is 32,000 bytes per second, about 1.9 MB per minute. At 48 kHz it is about 5.8 MB per minute. Stereo doubles these numbers.
